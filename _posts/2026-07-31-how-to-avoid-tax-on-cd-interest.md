Certificate of deposit interest is taxed as ordinary income by the IRS — and unlike some investments, you owe tax on it every year it's credited, even if the CD hasn't matured and you haven't touched a dollar of it. That surprises a lot of savers who assume tax only applies once they actually withdraw the money.

There's no legal way to make CD interest completely tax-free the way, say, a Roth IRA's growth is tax-free. But there are several legitimate strategies that can reduce, defer, or minimize the tax bill on your CD earnings. This guide covers how CD interest is actually taxed, and the realistic options available to lower what you owe.

## How CD Interest Is Taxed (The Basics)

Any bank or credit union that pays you $10 or more in CD interest during the year sends you a **Form 1099-INT**, and you're required to report that amount as ordinary income on your federal tax return — taxed at your regular marginal income tax rate, not the lower capital gains rate.

Two important details trip people up:

- **You're taxed annually, not just at maturity.** If you open a 3-year CD, you generally owe tax on the interest credited each of those three years, even though you can't access the money until the CD matures.
- **CD interest is taxed at both federal and state levels** (unless you live in a state with no income tax), unlike interest from U.S. Treasury bills and bonds, which is exempt from state and local tax.

Understanding this is the first step to reducing your CD tax bill — because most of the legitimate strategies below work by changing *where* your interest-bearing money sits, not by hiding the interest itself.

## 1. Hold CDs Inside a Traditional or Roth IRA

The single most effective way to shelter CD interest from tax is to buy the CD **inside a retirement account** rather than in a regular taxable account.

- **Traditional IRA CD:** Contributions may be tax-deductible, and the interest grows tax-deferred — you don't pay tax on it year by year. You're taxed only when you eventually withdraw funds in retirement.
- **Roth IRA CD:** Contributions are made with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals in retirement — including all the accumulated interest — are completely tax-free.

Many banks and brokerages offer IRA CDs specifically structured for this purpose. The trade-off is that IRA contribution limits apply, and early withdrawals before retirement age can trigger penalties, so this strategy works best for money you were planning to save long-term anyway.

## 2. Use an HSA if You're Saving for Medical Expenses

If part of your CD savings is earmarked for future medical costs, a **Health Savings Account (HSA)** offers triple tax benefits: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-deferred, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are entirely tax-free. Some HSA providers allow the cash balance to be placed in CD-like fixed-rate options.

## 3. Compare CDs Against Tax-Exempt or Tax-Advantaged Alternatives

Since CD interest is fully taxable at both federal and state levels, it's worth comparing the after-tax return against alternatives that carry a tax advantage:

- **U.S. Treasury bills and bonds** pay interest that's exempt from state and local income tax, though still taxable federally.
- **Municipal bonds** are generally exempt from federal tax, and often state tax too if you buy bonds issued by your own state.

For someone in a high state tax bracket, a lower-rate Treasury or municipal bond can sometimes produce a better after-tax return than a higher-rate CD, once state tax is factored in. This isn't "avoiding" tax on the CD itself — it's choosing an instrument that carries less tax burden in the first place.

## 4. Time CD Maturities to Manage Which Tax Year Interest Falls In

If you're close to a year where you expect to be in a lower tax bracket — for example, a year with reduced income, between jobs, or early retirement before other income sources kick in — timing when a CD matures or is renewed can shift some interest into that lower-bracket year, reducing the tax rate applied to it.

This is easier to plan with **short-term or no-penalty CDs**, since their maturity dates are more flexible to align with your tax situation, compared to locking into a 5-year CD with interest compounding in brackets you can't control years in advance.

## 5. Consider Series I Savings Bonds as a Tax-Deferred Alternative

**Series I Savings Bonds** from the U.S. Treasury let you defer federal tax on the interest until you cash the bond in or it matures (up to 30 years), rather than paying tax on it annually the way you do with a standard CD. I bonds are also exempt from state and local tax, and interest may be entirely tax-free if used for qualified higher education expenses, subject to income limits.

They're not a direct substitute for a CD — I bond rates are tied to inflation and there are annual purchase limits — but for money you don't need in the short term, they're worth comparing.

## 6. Use a Custodial Account for a Child's CD, Carefully

If a CD is held in a child's name through a custodial account (UGMA/UTMA), the interest may be taxed at the child's — typically lower — tax rate, up to certain limits, rather than the parent's rate. Above those limits, "kiddie tax" rules apply the parent's higher rate to the excess, so this strategy has diminishing returns for larger CD balances and is best suited to modest, education-focused savings rather than large deposits.

## 7. Offset CD Interest With Deductions, Not Exclusions

CD interest itself can't be offset by capital losses the way capital gains can — interest income and capital gains are taxed under different rules. But your overall tax liability, including tax on CD interest, is still reduced by any deductions and credits you're eligible for elsewhere on your return (retirement contributions, HSA contributions, itemized deductions, etc.). This won't make the CD interest itself tax-free, but it lowers your total taxable income, which reduces the effective rate applied to every dollar you earn — including your CD interest.

## What Doesn't Work

A few common misconceptions worth clearing up:

- **You can't avoid tax by not withdrawing the interest.** CDs are taxed on interest *credited*, not interest withdrawn, so leaving it in the account doesn't defer the tax bill.
- **Reinvesting interest into a new CD doesn't reset the tax clock.** It's still reported as income in the year it was earned.
- **Splitting a CD across multiple banks doesn't reduce total tax owed** — it just means you'll receive multiple 1099-INT forms reporting the same total interest.

## FAQ

### Is CD interest taxed even if I don't withdraw it?
Yes. CD interest is taxed as ordinary income in the year it's credited to your account, regardless of whether you withdraw it or let it stay until maturity.

### Can I avoid tax on CD interest completely?
Not entirely, under normal circumstances. However, holding the CD inside a Traditional IRA, Roth IRA, or HSA can defer or eliminate the tax, depending on the account type and how the funds are eventually used.

### Are CDs taxed at the state level?
Yes, in most states. Unlike U.S. Treasury securities, which are exempt from state and local tax, CD interest is generally taxable at both the federal and state level.

### Does a CD held in an IRA avoid taxes?
A Traditional IRA CD defers tax until withdrawal in retirement. A Roth IRA CD can make qualified withdrawals, including all accumulated interest, completely tax-free.

### What's a tax-free alternative to a CD?
Municipal bonds are generally exempt from federal tax (and often state tax, if issued by your home state). Series I Savings Bonds defer federal tax until redemption and may be tax-free if used for qualified education expenses.

## Glossary

**Form 1099-INT** — the tax form banks send reporting interest income of $10 or more paid during the year.

**Tax-Deferred** — growth that isn't taxed until funds are withdrawn, typically in retirement accounts.

**Tax-Exempt** — income that isn't subject to federal and/or state tax, such as municipal bond interest.

**Kiddie Tax** — IRS rules that tax a child's unearned income above a certain threshold at the parent's tax rate.

**Series I Savings Bond** — a U.S. Treasury bond with an inflation-linked rate and deferred federal tax treatment.
