<h2>How Much Home Can You Actually Afford?</h2>

<p>
  The amount a lender is willing to lend you is not necessarily the amount you should spend on a home. Your lender looks at your income, debts, credit history, assets, and other information to determine how much you qualify to borrow. Your own budget also needs to account for expenses that may not be fully reflected in a lender's qualification calculation.
</p>

<p>
  The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends looking beyond the mortgage itself when deciding what you can afford. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, homeowners association dues, maintenance, utilities, and other household expenses can all affect the amount that fits comfortably within your budget.
</p>

<p>
  This distinction matters because a larger approved loan does not automatically mean a larger loan is affordable. A useful affordability calculation starts with what you can comfortably pay each month and works backward toward a reasonable home price.
</p>

<h2>What Goes Into a Monthly Mortgage Payment?</h2>

<p>
  The principal and interest shown in a mortgage calculation are only part of the cost of owning a home. A typical monthly payment can also include property taxes, homeowners insurance, and mortgage insurance when it applies.
</p>

<p>
  The CFPB describes four common components of a mortgage payment: principal, interest, mortgage insurance, and property taxes and homeowners insurance. Taxes and insurance are technically costs of owning the property rather than costs of borrowing, but lenders often collect them through an escrow account along with the mortgage payment.
</p>

<p>
  This is why two homes with the same purchase price can have different monthly costs. Property-tax rates, insurance premiums, loan terms, down payments, and mortgage insurance requirements can all change the final amount.
</p>

<h2>How Your Interest Rate Changes the Cost of a Home</h2>

<p>
  The interest rate has a major effect on both your monthly payment and the total amount of interest paid over the life of the loan. A lower rate can reduce the cost of borrowing, while a higher rate can substantially increase the interest paid over several decades.
</p>

<p>
  Mortgage rates also change over time. Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey tracks average U.S. mortgage rates each week. For example, its September 10, 2026 survey reported an average 30-year fixed mortgage rate of 6.76% and a 15-year fixed rate of 6.09%. These are market averages, not rates guaranteed to any particular borrower.
</p>

<p>
  Your actual rate can differ from a national average because lenders consider factors such as credit history, loan type, loan-to-value ratio, property characteristics, and other borrower-specific information.
</p>

<h2>30-Year vs. 15-Year Mortgage</h2>

<p>
  A longer mortgage term generally produces a lower required monthly principal-and-interest payment, but you make payments for more years and can pay considerably more interest over the life of the loan.
</p>

<p>
  A shorter term usually increases the required monthly payment but reduces the number of years over which interest accrues. The right comparison therefore should not focus only on the monthly payment. Look at the total interest, the amount of principal paid down, and whether the higher payment fits comfortably within your budget.
</p>

<p>
  When comparing two mortgage terms, changing the term while keeping the loan amount and interest-rate assumptions visible makes the difference much easier to understand.
</p>

<h2>Fixed-Rate vs. Adjustable-Rate Mortgages</h2>

<p>
  A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the interest rate unchanged for the life of the loan, while an adjustable-rate mortgage can change according to the terms of the loan. An ARM may begin with a lower rate than a fixed-rate mortgage, but the payment can increase when the introductory period ends and the rate adjusts.
</p>

<p>
  When evaluating an ARM, the initial rate is only one part of the calculation. You should also look at when the first adjustment occurs, how frequently the rate can change, the index used to determine the rate, the applicable caps, and the maximum payment you could face under the loan terms.
</p>

<p>
  The CFPB specifically cautions borrowers not to assume they will automatically be able to sell the property or refinance before an ARM's rate changes. A change in property value or a change in your financial circumstances could make that strategy unavailable.
</p>

<h2>How Much Should You Put Down?</h2>

<p>
  A larger down payment reduces the amount you need to borrow, which can lower your monthly principal and interest payment and reduce the total interest paid. But putting more money into the home also means having less cash available for emergencies, moving expenses, repairs, and other financial goals.
</p>

<p>
  There is no single down-payment percentage that is appropriate for every buyer. The CFPB notes that down-payment requirements vary by loan type and lender. Borrowers who put less than 20% down on many conventional mortgages will typically have to pay private mortgage insurance, while some government-backed loan programs have different requirements.
</p>

<p>
  The cost of the down payment should therefore be considered together with closing costs and the cash you need to keep available after buying the property.
</p>

<h2>Don't Forget Closing Costs</h2>

<p>
  The money required to buy a home is not limited to the down payment. Closing costs can include lender charges, appraisal fees, title-related costs, government charges, prepaid interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and other expenses associated with completing the transaction.
</p>

<p>
  The CFPB estimates that closing costs typically range from about <strong>2% to 5% of the purchase price</strong>, excluding the down payment. The actual amount varies based on the loan, property, lender, location, and other factors.
</p>

<p>
  For example, on a $400,000 home, 2% to 5% would represent roughly $8,000 to $20,000 in closing costs before the down payment. This is only a planning range; your actual costs should come from the lender's Loan Estimate and other transaction documents.
</p>

<h2>Mortgage Insurance and PMI</h2>

<p>
  Mortgage insurance is designed primarily to protect the lender against the risk of borrower default. It does not protect the homeowner from losing the property or from other financial losses.
</p>

<p>
  On many conventional loans, borrowers who make a down payment of less than 20% will typically pay private mortgage insurance, although the exact requirements depend on the loan. FHA and USDA loans can also involve mortgage insurance under their respective program rules.
</p>

<p>
  Mortgage insurance adds to the cost of borrowing, so an affordability calculation should include it when applicable rather than comparing loans using principal and interest alone.
</p>

<h2>FHA, VA, USDA, and Conventional Mortgages</h2>

<p>
  Mortgage programs have different eligibility requirements, down-payment rules, insurance requirements, fees, and loan limits. A lower down payment does not necessarily mean a lower overall cost, so the comparison should include the full cost of the loan rather than focusing on one feature.
</p>

<p>
  FHA-insured mortgages can allow a minimum required investment of 3.5% in many circumstances, according to HUD. The exact amount depends on the borrower's circumstances and applicable FHA requirements.
</p>

<p>
  VA and USDA loans have their own eligibility requirements and program rules. Rather than assuming that one loan type is always better, compare the amount you need to put down, the interest rate, mortgage insurance or guarantee fees, closing costs, and the total cost over the period you expect to keep the loan.
</p>

<h2>Conforming Loan Limits</h2>

<p>
  The size of a mortgage can affect which loan programs are available. Loans that meet the requirements for purchase by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are generally referred to as conforming loans, while larger loans can fall outside the standard conforming limits and may be treated as jumbo loans.
</p>

<p>
  For 2026, the baseline conforming loan limit for a one-unit property in most U.S. counties is <strong>$832,750</strong>. The ceiling in high-cost areas is <strong>$1,249,125</strong> for one-unit properties. The applicable limit can be higher in certain areas and differs for properties with more than one unit.
</p>

<p>
  Loan limits are not the same as an affordability limit. Being below a conforming loan limit does not mean that the loan is affordable for a particular borrower.
</p>

<h2>Refinancing a Mortgage</h2>

<p>
  Refinancing replaces an existing mortgage with a new loan. Borrowers may consider refinancing when interest rates change, when they want a different loan term, or when they want to change other features of their financing.
</p>

<p>
  A lower interest rate does not automatically make refinancing worthwhile. The new loan can involve closing costs and other fees, so the savings need to be large enough and last long enough to recover those costs.
</p>

<p>
  A simple way to evaluate a refinance is to compare the expected monthly savings with the total refinancing costs. If refinancing costs $6,000 and reduces the payment by $250 per month, the simple break-even period would be 24 months. That calculation does not account for changes in the loan term, taxes, interest paid over the life of the new loan, or other costs, so it should be treated as a starting point rather than a complete analysis.
</p>

<h2>Home Equity Loans and HELOCs</h2>

<p>
  Home equity is generally the difference between the value of your home and the amount you owe against it. Homeowners can potentially borrow against that equity through a home equity loan or a home equity line of credit (HELOC).
</p>

<p>
  A home equity loan generally provides a specific amount of money, while a HELOC provides a revolving line that can be borrowed and repaid during the draw period. HELOCs usually have adjustable interest rates, so payments can change as rates or the outstanding balance change.
</p>

<p>
  Both forms of borrowing use the home as security. If you cannot repay the debt, you can put the property at risk. The CFPB also notes that taking out a HELOC can affect your ability to refinance the first mortgage and may require approval from the HELOC lender.
</p>

<h2>How to Compare a Mortgage Before You Commit</h2>

<p>
  A mortgage comparison should look at more than the advertised interest rate. The CFPB recommends considering the loan amount, interest rate, points, closing costs, APR, loan type, loan term, and potentially risky loan features when comparing mortgages.
</p>

<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>Start with the home price:</strong>
    Determine the purchase price you are considering and the amount you can comfortably use for the down payment.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Calculate the loan amount:</strong>
    Subtract the down payment from the purchase price. Keep closing costs and other cash requirements separate from the down payment.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Compare interest rates:</strong>
    Use the actual rate offered to you when possible rather than relying on a national average. Market surveys such as Freddie Mac's PMMS are useful for understanding broader rate trends.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Include the full monthly housing cost:</strong>
    Add principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and applicable HOA costs rather than looking only at principal and interest.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Calculate upfront costs:</strong>
    Include the down payment, closing costs, prepaid expenses, and the cash you want to keep available after closing. The CFPB notes that closing costs commonly fall in the 2%–5% range, although actual costs vary.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Compare the total cost:</strong>
    Look at how much interest you would pay over the loan term, not just the monthly payment.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Stress-test the payment:</strong>
    Consider what happens if taxes, insurance, or other housing expenses increase. For an ARM, also test a higher future interest rate.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Review the lender's documents:</strong>
    Once you apply, use the Loan Estimate and other disclosures to compare the actual loan terms and costs rather than relying solely on an online estimate. The CFPB's Loan Estimate guidance explains how to review the estimated total monthly payment and cash required at closing.
  </li>
</ol>

<h2>Mortgage &amp; Real Estate Data Sources</h2>

<p>
  Mortgage rates, loan limits, government-program rules, insurance requirements, and closing costs can change. The following sources are useful for checking the information behind mortgage calculations.
</p>

<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB):</strong>
    Provides official consumer guidance on mortgage affordability, loan estimates, closing costs, mortgage insurance, fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages, refinancing, and home-equity products.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey:</strong>
    Publishes weekly U.S. mortgage-rate data, including average 30-year and 15-year fixed mortgage rates. The figures are market averages and are not guaranteed offers for individual borrowers.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA):</strong>
    Publishes annual conforming loan limits and information used to determine the maximum mortgage balances eligible for purchase by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD):</strong>
    Provides official information about FHA-insured mortgages, including eligibility, minimum required investment, and FHA program requirements.
  </li>
</ul>

<p>
  Mortgage calculations are estimates based on the information entered and should not be treated as a loan offer, approval, appraisal, or individualized financial advice. Actual mortgage terms depend on the lender, borrower, property, loan program, location, and applicable rules. Always verify the final terms and costs using your lender's disclosures and the applicable government or program source.
</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>How much house can I afford?</h3>

<p>
  The amount you can afford depends on your income, existing debts, down payment, mortgage rate, taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, other housing costs, and the amount of cash you need to keep available after buying. The amount a lender will approve is not necessarily the amount that fits comfortably within your budget.
</p>

<h3>How much should I put down on a house?</h3>

<p>
  There is no single down-payment percentage that works for every buyer. A larger down payment reduces the amount borrowed, while a smaller down payment leaves more cash available for other needs but can result in mortgage insurance and a larger loan balance.
</p>

<h3>How much are mortgage closing costs?</h3>

<p>
  Closing costs commonly range from about 2% to 5% of the home's purchase price, excluding the down payment, according to the CFPB. Your actual costs depend on the property, loan, lender, location, and other transaction details.
</p>

<h3>What is included in a mortgage payment?</h3>

<p>
  A mortgage payment can include principal, interest, mortgage insurance, property taxes, and homeowners insurance. HOA dues and some other property costs may be paid separately.
</p>

<h3>Is a 15-year mortgage better than a 30-year mortgage?</h3>

<p>
  Neither term is automatically better. A 15-year mortgage generally requires higher monthly payments but can reduce the total interest paid, while a 30-year mortgage generally has a lower required payment but spreads repayment over a longer period. Compare both the monthly payment and total borrowing cost.
</p>

<h3>What is the difference between a fixed-rate mortgage and an ARM?</h3>

<p>
  A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the interest rate unchanged, while an adjustable-rate mortgage can change according to the loan's terms. An ARM may start with a lower rate, but its payment can increase after the initial fixed period.
</p>

<h3>Do I need 20% down to buy a house?</h3>

<p>
  No. Some mortgage programs allow substantially smaller down payments. However, putting less than 20% down on many conventional mortgages typically results in private mortgage insurance, which increases the cost of the loan.
</p>

<h3>What is PMI?</h3>

<p>
  Private mortgage insurance, or PMI, is insurance that generally protects the lender when a conventional mortgage has a relatively small down payment. It does not protect the homeowner against losing the property or other financial losses.
</p>

<h3>Should I refinance my mortgage?</h3>

<p>
  Refinancing can make sense when the savings or other benefits of the new loan justify the refinancing costs. Compare the new rate and payment with the total closing costs, remaining loan term, and total interest rather than judging the refinance only by the monthly payment.
</p>

<h3>What is a HELOC?</h3>

<p>
  A HELOC is a revolving line of credit secured by your home's equity. You can generally borrow repeatedly during the draw period, and HELOCs usually have adjustable interest rates, so payments can change. Failure to repay the debt can put the home at risk.
</p>

<h3>What is the 2026 conforming loan limit?</h3>

<p>
  The 2026 baseline conforming loan limit for a one-unit property in most U.S. counties is $832,750. High-cost areas can have a higher limit, with a one-unit ceiling of $1,249,125 under the 2026 limits.
</p>

<h3>What are mortgage rates right now?</h3>

<p>
  Mortgage rates change regularly. Freddie Mac's weekly Primary Mortgage Market Survey is a useful reference for average U.S. mortgage rates. For September 10, 2026, Freddie Mac reported an average 30-year fixed rate of 6.76% and a 15-year fixed rate of 6.09%. Your actual rate may be different because lenders price loans based on the borrower's and property's characteristics.
</p>
