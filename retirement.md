<h2>How Much Do You Need to Retire?</h2>

<p>
  There is no single retirement savings number that works for everyone. The amount you need depends on when you plan to retire, how much you expect to spend, how much income you will receive from sources such as Social Security or a pension, how long your retirement may last, and how your investments perform.
</p>

<p>
  That is why a retirement calculation should start with your expected spending rather than a target account balance. If you expect to spend $60,000 a year in retirement, for example, you need to consider how much of that amount could come from Social Security, pensions, or other reliable income and how much would need to come from your savings.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>Retirement Calculator</strong> can help estimate how your current savings and future contributions could grow toward a retirement target. You can change the retirement age, contribution amount, expected return, and other assumptions to see how the result changes. The goal is not to produce a guaranteed retirement date, but to show how sensitive the outcome is to the numbers you enter.
</p>

<h2>Why Retirement Calculators Give Different Results</h2>

<p>
  Two retirement calculators can give very different answers even when you enter the same salary and account balance. The difference usually comes from the assumptions used behind the calculation.
</p>

<p>
  Common differences include the expected investment return, inflation rate, retirement age, contribution growth, employer matching, retirement spending, Social Security income, taxes, and the length of retirement. Some calculators also treat contributions as monthly deposits while others use annual contributions.
</p>

<p>
  A useful retirement calculation should therefore show its assumptions rather than presenting one number as a prediction. If changing the expected return from 5% to 7% dramatically changes the result, that difference is important information in itself.
</p>

<p>
  Investor.gov provides retirement and investment education resources and explains that diversification can reduce the risk associated with relying heavily on a single investment, although diversification cannot eliminate investment losses.
</p>

<h2>401(k), 403(b), and Retirement Contribution Limits</h2>

<p>
  The amount you can contribute to a retirement plan is subject to annual limits, and those limits can change from year to year. For 2026, the basic employee elective-deferral limit for most 401(k) and 403(b) plans is <strong>$24,500</strong>. The general catch-up contribution for employees age 50 and older is <strong>$8,000</strong>, while employees who are age 60, 61, 62, or 63 during 2026 may qualify for the higher <strong>$11,250</strong> catch-up limit.
</p>

<p>
  These limits apply to the employee contribution side of the plan. There are also overall limits that can include employer contributions. For 2026, the defined-contribution plan annual additions limit is generally $72,000, subject to the rules and exceptions described by the IRS.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>401(k) Calculator</strong>, <strong>403(b) Calculator</strong>, <strong>Solo 401(k) Contribution Calculator</strong>, and <strong>Catch-Up Contribution Calculator</strong> can help estimate contributions and long-term account growth. Because contribution limits and tax rules can change, the IRS should be treated as the authoritative source for the current year's limits.
</p>

<h2>Traditional IRA and Roth IRA Planning</h2>

<p>
  Traditional and Roth IRAs have different tax treatment, and the right calculation depends on the account rules that apply to your situation. A traditional IRA may provide a tax deduction when contributions are eligible, while qualified Roth IRA withdrawals are generally tax-free.
</p>

<p>
  For 2026, the combined contribution limit for traditional and Roth IRAs is <strong>$7,500</strong>, or <strong>$8,600</strong> for individuals age 50 or older, subject to the individual's taxable compensation and other applicable rules. The limit applies across your traditional and Roth IRAs rather than giving you a separate $7,500 limit for each account.
</p>

<p>
  Eligibility and deductibility can also depend on income and whether you or your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan. The IRS publishes the applicable income ranges and contribution rules each year.
</p>

<p>
  Use the <strong>IRA Calculator</strong>, <strong>Roth IRA Calculator</strong>, and <strong>Backdoor Roth IRA Calculator</strong> to compare the numbers under different assumptions. These calculators are estimates and should not be treated as tax advice, particularly when eligibility, conversions, or tax consequences depend on details outside the calculator inputs.
</p>

<h2>When Can You Retire?</h2>

<p>
  Retirement age is not determined by your account balance alone. You may be financially ready to stop working when your expected retirement income and savings can cover your spending for the period you expect to be retired.
</p>

<p>
  Starting with your desired retirement age, estimate annual spending and subtract income you expect from Social Security, pensions, rental income, or other sources. The remaining amount is what your portfolio may need to provide.
</p>

<p>
  The earlier you retire, the more years your savings may need to support you. Early retirement can also create a longer period before Social Security and Medicare become available, depending on your circumstances. That makes the assumptions about healthcare costs, investment returns, withdrawals, and longevity particularly important.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>Retirement Calculator</strong>, <strong>FIRE Calculator</strong>, <strong>Coast FIRE Calculator</strong>, and <strong>Barista FIRE Calculator</strong> are designed for different versions of this question. Instead of treating the result as a definitive retirement date, use the calculators to compare how changes in savings, spending, investment returns, and retirement age affect the projected outcome.
</p>

<h2>Social Security and Retirement Age</h2>

<p>
  Social Security can be an important part of retirement income, but the age at which you claim benefits affects the amount you receive. The earliest age for retirement benefits is currently 62. For people attaining age 62 in 2026, the full retirement age is 67. Your full retirement age depends on your year of birth.
</p>

<p>
  Claiming before full retirement age generally results in a permanent reduction in the monthly benefit. Waiting beyond full retirement age can increase the monthly benefit, with delayed retirement credits available until age 70.
</p>

<p>
  Social Security also uses your highest 35 years of earnings when calculating retirement benefits. If you stop working with fewer than 35 years of earnings, years without earnings can reduce the calculation.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>Social Security Break-Even Calculator</strong> and <strong>Social Security Spousal Benefit Calculator</strong> can help compare different claiming scenarios. For an individual's actual benefit estimate, however, the Social Security Administration's own records and benefit estimates should take priority over a general calculator.
</p>

<h2>How Much Can You Withdraw in Retirement?</h2>

<p>
  Accumulating retirement savings is only part of the problem. Once you stop working, you need a withdrawal strategy that balances current spending with the possibility of living many years in retirement.
</p>

<p>
  A withdrawal rate is the amount withdrawn from a portfolio in relation to its starting value. For example, withdrawing $40,000 from a $1 million portfolio represents an initial withdrawal rate of 4%.
</p>

<p>
  A commonly discussed starting point is the 4% rule, but it should not be treated as a guarantee that a portfolio will last for every retiree. The appropriate withdrawal rate depends on factors such as retirement length, portfolio composition, market returns, inflation, spending changes, and whether leaving money to heirs is a priority.
</p>

<p>
  Current Vanguard retirement-income research suggests that a withdrawal rate of roughly <strong>3.5%–4%</strong> can support a 30-year retirement for many households under its modeled assumptions. Vanguard also emphasizes that these projections are hypothetical and are not guarantees of future investment results.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>4% Rule Calculator</strong> and <strong>Retirement Withdrawal Calculator</strong> let you test different withdrawal rates, portfolio values, investment returns, and retirement periods. Running several scenarios is more informative than treating one withdrawal percentage as universally safe.
</p>

<h2>Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)</h2>

<p>
  Retirement planning does not end when you stop working. Traditional retirement accounts are subject to required minimum distribution rules, which determine when minimum withdrawals generally have to begin.
</p>

<p>
  Under current federal rules, owners of traditional IRAs, SEP IRAs, and SIMPLE IRAs generally must begin taking RMDs at age 73. For many employer retirement plans, the required beginning date can depend on both age and whether the person is still working, subject to the plan and ownership rules.
</p>

<p>
  The first RMD can generally be delayed until April 1 of the year following the year you reach the applicable starting age. However, delaying the first distribution means you may have two RMDs due in that following calendar year. Subsequent RMDs are generally due by December 31 each year.
</p>

<p>
  RMD amounts are generally calculated using the account balance from the previous December 31 and an applicable IRS life-expectancy factor. Missing an RMD can result in an excise tax, although the law provides circumstances in which the penalty can be reduced or corrected.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>RMD Calculator</strong> and <strong>Inherited IRA RMD Calculator</strong> can help estimate required distributions. Because RMD rules are detailed and have changed in recent years, users should verify the result against the current IRS rules for their specific account and beneficiary situation.
</p>

<h2>Inherited IRAs and Retirement Accounts</h2>

<p>
  Inheriting a retirement account can create a different set of distribution rules from those that apply to the original account owner. The rules depend on when the owner died, the type of account, and the relationship and status of the beneficiary.
</p>

<p>
  For many non-spouse beneficiaries, the SECURE Act's 10-year rule requires the inherited account to be emptied by the end of the tenth year following the owner's death, although exceptions and additional distribution requirements can apply. Eligible designated beneficiaries, including certain surviving spouses, minor children, disabled or chronically ill individuals, and beneficiaries who are not more than 10 years younger than the original owner, can have different options.
</p>

<p>
  Inherited Roth IRAs also have distribution rules, even though the original Roth IRA owner is generally not subject to RMDs during their lifetime. The tax treatment of inherited distributions depends on the account and beneficiary circumstances.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>Inherited IRA RMD Calculator</strong> can help with an initial estimate, but inherited-account rules can be complicated enough that the IRS guidance or a qualified tax professional should be consulted when the calculation affects a real distribution decision.
</p>

<h2>Healthcare Costs Can Change the Retirement Calculation</h2>

<p>
  Healthcare is one of the expenses that can make retirement planning difficult to estimate. Your healthcare costs may change substantially depending on when you retire, whether you have employer coverage, when you become eligible for Medicare, the type of coverage you choose, and your medical needs.
</p>

<p>
  Medicare eligibility generally begins around age 65, while Social Security's full retirement age can be different. Retirement planning should therefore avoid treating the two ages as interchangeable.
</p>

<p>
  The <strong>Retirement Healthcare Cost Calculator</strong> can be used to model estimated healthcare expenses as part of a larger retirement budget. The result is only as reliable as the assumptions entered, so current insurance premiums, Medicare information, and personal circumstances should be reviewed separately.
</p>

<h2>How to Build a Retirement Calculation</h2>

<p>
  A practical retirement calculation starts with the parts of your financial situation that you can estimate and then tests how the result changes when those assumptions change.
</p>

<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>Choose a target retirement age:</strong>
    Start with the age at which you would like to stop working or reduce your working hours. Then consider whether your savings and expected income could support that timeline.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Estimate retirement spending:</strong>
    Separate essential expenses from discretionary spending where possible. Housing, food, insurance, healthcare, taxes, travel, and other recurring costs can all affect the amount your portfolio needs to provide.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>List other retirement income:</strong>
    Include expected Social Security, pensions, rental income, annuity payments, or other recurring income. Social Security estimates should be based on your own earnings record rather than a generic assumption whenever possible.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Enter current retirement savings:</strong>
    Include your 401(k), IRA, taxable investments, and other assets that you actually intend to use for retirement. Keep account types separate when taxes or withdrawal rules make a difference.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Enter future contributions:</strong>
    Estimate how much you expect to contribute each month or year. Include employer contributions where the calculator supports them, but do not assume an employer match unless your plan actually provides one.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Use a reasonable investment-return assumption:</strong>
    No calculator can know the return your portfolio will earn in the future. Rather than relying on one optimistic number, test several return assumptions and see whether the conclusion changes materially.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Account for inflation:</strong>
    A retirement balance expressed in future dollars does not have the same purchasing power as the same number today. Include inflation when comparing future retirement income with today's spending.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Test different retirement ages:</strong>
    Compare retiring at different ages instead of assuming one date is fixed. A few additional working years can mean additional contributions, more time for investments to grow, and fewer years that the portfolio needs to support.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Test different withdrawal rates:</strong>
    Compare conservative and higher withdrawal assumptions. Vanguard's current research places roughly 3.5%–4% within the range that can support a 30-year retirement for many households under its modeled assumptions, but individual circumstances can require a different approach.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Review the result periodically:</strong>
    Retirement planning is not a one-time calculation. Account balances, contributions, market conditions, inflation, tax rules, Social Security estimates, and spending can all change.
  </li>
</ol>

<h2>Retirement Planning Data and Reference Sources</h2>

<p>
  Retirement calculations involve tax rules, government benefits, contribution limits, and investment assumptions that can change. The following sources provide primary or established research that can be used to verify the information behind these calculations.
</p>

<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Internal Revenue Service (IRS):</strong>
    The IRS is the primary source for federal retirement-account contribution limits, IRA rules, RMD requirements, catch-up contributions, inherited retirement-account rules, and related tax requirements. The 2026 401(k) employee deferral limit is $24,500 and the IRA contribution limit is $7,500.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Social Security Administration (SSA):</strong>
    SSA provides the official rules for Social Security retirement eligibility, full retirement age, claiming ages, benefit calculations, and individual benefit estimates. Full retirement age is 67 for people born in 1960 or later, while retirement benefits can generally begin as early as age 62 with a reduction for claiming early.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Investor.gov:</strong>
    The SEC's Investor.gov provides investor education covering compound growth, diversification, asset allocation, retirement planning, and investment risk. Its guidance emphasizes that diversification can reduce risk but cannot eliminate investment losses.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Vanguard research:</strong>
    Vanguard publishes retirement-income research and long-term investment analysis. Its 2026 retirement-income research discusses withdrawal rates, retirement spending, longevity, and the risks involved in turning accumulated savings into sustainable income. Its projections are hypothetical and should not be treated as guarantees.
  </li>

  <li>
    <strong>Medicare:</strong>
    Medicare.gov provides official information about Medicare eligibility, enrollment, premiums, coverage, and healthcare costs that may affect retirement planning. Medicare eligibility generally begins at age 65, which is separate from Social Security full retirement age.
  </li>
</ul>

<p>
  Retirement rules and limits can change as legislation, regulations, and annual cost-of-living adjustments change. Wanjaaro's calculators are intended for estimation and comparison, not individualized financial, investment, tax, or legal advice. Before acting on a result involving a large contribution, retirement-account conversion, withdrawal, Social Security claim, or inherited account, verify the applicable rules with the relevant government source or a qualified professional.
</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>How much money do I need to retire?</h3>
<p>
  There is no universal retirement number. The amount you need depends mainly on your expected retirement spending, other income such as Social Security or a pension, retirement age, investment returns, inflation, taxes, and how long your savings need to last.
</p>

<h3>Why do retirement calculators give different answers?</h3>
<p>
  Retirement calculators can give different answers because they use different assumptions for investment returns, inflation, contributions, retirement spending, taxes, Social Security, and life expectancy. Always compare the assumptions before comparing the results.
</p>

<h3>How much should I contribute to my 401(k)?</h3>
<p>
  The right contribution depends on your income, retirement goal, employer match, current savings, and other financial priorities. For 2026, the employee contribution limit for most 401(k) plans is $24,500, with additional catch-up contributions available to eligible older workers.
</p>

<h3>What is the 4% rule for retirement?</h3>
<p>
  The 4% rule is a retirement-withdrawal guideline based on taking about 4% of an initial portfolio in the first year and adjusting withdrawals for inflation in subsequent years. It is not a guarantee that your money will last. Current Vanguard research suggests roughly 3.5%–4% can support a 30-year retirement for many households under its modeled assumptions.
</p>

<h3>When can I start receiving Social Security retirement benefits?</h3>
<p>
  Social Security retirement benefits can generally begin at age 62. Starting before full retirement age reduces the monthly benefit, while delaying benefits after full retirement age can increase the monthly amount up to age 70.
</p>

<h3>What is full retirement age?</h3>
<p>
  Full retirement age depends on your birth year. For people attaining age 62 in 2026, the full retirement age is 67. For people born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is also 67.
</p>

<h3>When do I have to start taking RMDs?</h3>
<p>
  Under current rules, traditional IRA owners generally must begin required minimum distributions at age 73. The rules for employer retirement plans can depend on whether you are still working and other plan-specific circumstances.
</p>

<h3>Can I retire before 65?</h3>
<p>
  Yes, but retiring before 65 can require additional planning because Medicare eligibility generally begins at 65. You may also need to fund more years without employment income or Social Security benefits, depending on when you claim them.
</p>

<h3>How much can I withdraw from my retirement savings each year?</h3>
<p>
  There is no withdrawal amount that is guaranteed to be safe for every retiree. A withdrawal rate should be considered alongside retirement length, portfolio allocation, inflation, market returns, spending changes, and other income. Vanguard's current research uses roughly 3.5%–4% as a reference range for many 30-year retirement scenarios, not as a universal rule.
</p>

<h3>What happens to an IRA when the owner dies?</h3>
<p>
  The distribution rules depend on the beneficiary, the account type, and when the owner died. Many non-spouse beneficiaries are subject to a 10-year distribution rule, while eligible designated beneficiaries can have different options.
</p>

<h3>Are Roth IRAs subject to RMDs?</h3>
<p>
  An original Roth IRA owner generally does not have to take RMDs during their lifetime. Beneficiaries of inherited Roth IRAs are generally subject to distribution rules after the owner's death.
</p>

<h3>Should I use one retirement calculator or several?</h3>
<p>
  Using several calculators can be useful when they answer different questions. A retirement calculator can estimate whether your savings may support a target retirement, while separate tools can examine 401(k) contributions, IRA contributions, Social Security, withdrawal rates, RMDs, healthcare costs, or early-retirement scenarios.
</p>
